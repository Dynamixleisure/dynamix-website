Manage the Boulevard Like a Theme Park

The Gulf has built the world’s most ambitious destinations. Most are still run like lease books. The numbers that turned around a three-park portfolio in Doha apply to every waterfront, boulevard and mixed-use district in the region.

The hardware is finished. The operating model is not.

Walk any new destination in the Gulf – a marina promenade, a retail boulevard, a cultural district – and you will see world-class hardware: the paving, the lighting, the water features, the anchor brands. Then ask the asset manager how the place performed last month and you will hear occupancy, rent per square metre and footfall. Those three numbers describe a lease book. They do not describe a destination, and the gap between the two is where billions of dirhams and riyals of invested capital are quietly under-earning.

I have spent twenty years on the other side of that gap, running theme parks, family entertainment centres and integrated-resort attractions from Dubai and Abu Dhabi to South East Asia and Doha, and more recently estates, hotels and mixed-use assets. The attractions industry learned long ago that the gate is not the business. The business is what happens in the three or four hours after it. Real estate is only now discovering the same truth, and it has the harder version of the problem, because a boulevard has no gate at all.

Three numbers that are not on the dashboard.

A theme park lives and dies by three measures that rarely appear in an asset manager’s monthly pack. The first is per-cap spend – total revenue divided by the number of guests who came through. The second is dwell time, the hours a guest actually stays. The third is recency, the gap between one visit and the next. Together they describe the only thing that ultimately matters to an owner: how much each visitor is worth over a lifetime, and how fast that value compounds.

Footfall, by contrast, is a vanity metric when it stands alone. Two years ago, my team and I took responsibility for a three-park portfolio whose footfall was respectable and whose economics were not. We did not have capital for new attractions. What we had was pricing authority, a content calendar and the data from an integrated ticketing platform. Over two years aggregate per-cap spend rose from QAR 75 to QAR 180 per visit, and the share of each guest’s spend captured inside the gates grew from roughly a fifth to two thirds or more. In the year the regional market lost around a fifth of its demand and our own attendance fell by some hundred thousand visitors, revenue rose by more than QAR 7 million. Nothing was built. The yield was managed.

The three-and-a-half-hour rule

The most useful single number I know in destination management is a length of stay of between three and a half and four hours. Below it, the guest has not reached the second meal, the second purchase or the moment of ease in which the wallet opens. Above it, fatigue sets in, per-hour spend falls away, and – this is the part owners miss – the appetite to return soon falls with it. A family that stays six hours has done the place; a family that leaves after four hours, pleasantly unfinished, comes back within weeks. Managing to that window is how you lift per-cap and recency at the same time rather than trading one for the other.

Translate that to a mixed-use district and the implications are immediate. The question is no longer how many people crossed the threshold on Friday, but how long they stayed, how many of them ate, parked, shopped and watched something, and when they were last here. Those are design questions, programming questions and pricing questions – and every one of them sits with the owner, not the tenant.

The content calendar is a revenue instrument.

In most property companies, events sit in the marketing budget as a cost. In a well-run park, the content calendar is the revenue engine, planned twelve months ahead and read by the finance director as closely as the rent roll. Content gives marketing something to push; the call to action puts the right value at the right price in front of the right segment; the visit delivers the in-park spend. Off-peak weeks are filled by programming designed for the segment that has time – schools, families with young children, residents – and distributed through the channels that reach them. Peak periods are not simply enjoyed; they are harvested, with activations placed where the crowd is thickest and spend is easiest.

A boulevard can be run the same way. A quiet Tuesday in August is not weather; it is an unprogrammed asset. A Friday evening in February is not luck; it is the moment to layer the premium experience, the paid activation and the partner showcase. The owners who treat their event calendar as an operating plan, with revenue targets per week, consistently outperform those who treat it as decoration.

Real estate has pricing too – it just calls it something else.

Attractions people are taught differential pricing from their first week: peak and off-peak, seasonal, early-bird, online against gate, bundles, passes, and the rebate structures that make online travel agents and destination management companies work for you in the weeks you need them. Property people tend to believe pricing is the tenant’s business. It is not. Turnover rent is a pricing instrument. Parking tariffs by hour and by day are a pricing instrument. Resident and visitor passes, memberships, paid experiences, event tickets and partner access are all pricing instruments. A destination that does not own a view of the price its visitor pays across all of them has handed the yield to someone else.

The sponsor and partner channel deserves a particular word. The best destination deals we have done – with a nationalized telecom operator, with the national tourism authority – were never value-in-kind arrangements. The doctrine was always maximum cash plus institutional support: the partner’s media, PR and digital reach delivered as a contractual obligation alongside the fee, never instead of it. Owners who accept amplification in place of money are subsidising their partner’s marketing with their own asset.

You cannot yield-manage what you cannot see.

None of this works on a monthly report. A park with integrated ticketing knows by noon what the day will do. A mixed-use district has no ticket, but it has parking systems, Wi-Fi, loyalty programmes, tenant point-of-sale feeds and the leasing data itself – and almost nobody joins them up. We did it with business intelligence dashboards first, and then connected corporate AI models directly to the live data so that they could flag the trend and the anomaly a human analyst would miss: the restaurant whose Thursday fell for three weeks running, the car park that filled an hour earlier than the footfall counters suggested. Those dashboards became the instrument senior leadership and stakeholders used to take decisions, and they were built by repurposing people, not replacing them. Month-end close fell from fifteen days to five, which meant the pricing decision for next weekend could be made with last weekend’s facts.

Monday morning

If I were handed a destination asset tomorrow, I would do five things before touching the capex plan. I would establish per-cap, dwell time and recency as the three headline measures beside rent and occupancy and report them weekly. I would take ownership of a twelve-month content calendar with a revenue target for every week, not just a budget line. I would map every price the visitor pays across the estate – parking, passes, experiences, events, partners – and manage them as one architecture. I would connect the data the estate already generates into a single live view and let the machines look for what people miss. And I would set the three-and-a-half-hour target and design everything, from seating to shade to the sequence of offers, to reach it.

Only then would I ask what to build. In my experience the answer is usually less than the board expects, because the yield was there all along.

A destination is an operating business that happens to own land.

The Gulf’s great advantage is that it has already made the hard investment. The paving is laid, the brands are signed, the visitors are coming. The next decade of value in the region’s destinations will not come from the next tower. It will come from running the place between the towers the way the best parks have always been run – by the guest, by the hour, by the riyal – and from owners who understand that an asset manager’s job is not to collect the rent, but to make the place worth more every time someone walks through it.
